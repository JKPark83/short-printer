# Sustaining Curiosity After the Hook in 60–90 s Explainer Shorts (open loops, re-hooks, "but" turns, payoff timing)

Scope note: theory (Loewenstein, Kang, Heath) + field evidence (Upworthy A/B archive analyses, question-headline studies) + creator practice (Kallaway, Jenny Hoyos, MrBeast guide, Veritasium/Derek Muller, Sugarman). Templates in Q1 are the researcher's abstraction of sourced techniques; the mechanism column is sourced, the English example lines are written for illustration unless quoted.

## Q1. What are the strongest 10–15 hook formulas (abstract templates), and what mechanism does each exploit?

### Takeaway
Every durable formula is a way to make the viewer aware of a *specific, closeable* gap in something they already half-know (Loewenstein), usually by contradicting an expectation (Heath "Unexpected", Kallaway "contrast") and using forward reference ("this", "the real reason") rather than an overt tutorial/question cue. The field data says moderate specificity wins: vague teasers and fully-explicit summaries both lose.

### Cited Findings
**Theory that the formulas exploit**
- Curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge. Such information gaps produce the feeling of deprivation labeled curiosity." The gap requires some prior knowledge and must seem closeable — complete ignorance does not trigger curiosity. — [PBS NOVA, "How to Stimulate Curiosity" (on Loewenstein 1994)](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/); [Nautilus, "Curiosity depends on what you already know"](https://nautil.us/curiosity-depends-on-what-you-already-know-235803)
- Kang et al. 2009 (Psych Science, n=19 fMRI, 40 trivia Qs): curiosity correlated with caudate (anticipated-reward) activity; when participants guessed *wrong*, curiosity increased activity in memory regions, and higher curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers 1–2 weeks later. (The paper also reports curiosity as an inverted-U function of confidence — highest when people half-know.) — [PubMed 19619181](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/); [Berkeley summary](https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/papers/papers_files/Kang_PsychSci_2009.html)
- Made to Stick ("Unexpected" chapter): create/exploit a gap by suggesting there's something important they don't know *before* giving answers; methods: point to missing knowledge, pose puzzles, assert others know something the audience doesn't, ask for predictions; make people commit to a flawed idea before refuting it (to beat overconfidence). Saturn-rings "mystery story" (Cialdini): pose the question, walk through failed theories, reveal "dust". Cialdini: "Mysteries are powerful because they create a need for closure." — [Shortform summary of Made to Stick](https://www.shortform.com/blog/create-interest); [Made to Stick PDF](https://eclass.physics.uoc.gr/modules/document/file.php/PH103/Made_To_Stick_PDF%20%281%29.pdf)
- Upworthy archive (22,743 RCTs, ~105k headline variants, 370M impressions; Banerjee & Urminsky, Marketing Science 2025): **forward-reference** language (unresolved pronouns, "This is what happened when he said sorry!") *increased* CTR; **numbers** increased CTR; **more concrete** headlines increased CTR; headlines implying **social stories about individuals** did better; more **emotionally intense** language helped; but **information-seeking cues (interrogative/tutorial words, question marks — "suggesting the content would answer a question or teach something") performed worse**, and **headlines directed at the reader ("you") backfired**; mentions of money reduced CTR. Prior research/industry advice was often wrong in *direction* (Bonferroni model: 5 effects in predicted direction, 4 opposite). — [Banerjee & Urminsky working paper PDF](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf); [Marketing Science listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v44y2025i3p566-592.html)
- Aubin Le Quéré & Matias (Scientific Reports 2025; 8,977 Upworthy A/B tests, 35,910 headlines): concreteness has a curvilinear effect — when competing headlines are vague, more concreteness raises clicks; when already concrete, more concreteness *lowers* clicks by up to 9.9%. Interpreted via information-gap theory: moderate uncertainty maximizes curiosity. Example poles: "This Will Change How You Think About Politics" (vague) vs "Kamala Harris Visits New York" (concrete). — [PMC11704130](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11704130/)
- Negativity (Robertson et al., Nature Human Behaviour 2023, same Upworthy data): each additional negative word raised CTR by 2.3% (avg-length headline); each positive word lowered it 1.0%; sadness raised clicks, joy and fear lowered them, anger n.s. — [PMC10202797](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/)

**Creator formulas (sourced)**
- Kallaway 3-step hook: (1) *Context lean-in* (topic clarity + common ground/benefit/pain/mind-blowing fact), (2) *Scroll-stop interjection* — one "stun gun" line with "but / however / yet / although", (3) *Contrarian snapback* — a surprising statement moving opposite to the lean-in but on-topic; bigger contrast = stronger. Verbatim: "The tech in the Vegas sphere is insane, biggest screen ever built, 20 times bigger than an IMAX, but get this, the screen is actually the least impressive part of the whole thing because the most impressive part is the audio." And: "There are three massive mistakes people are making with their mortgage... Now most people think it's because of high interest rates, but it turns out it has nothing to do with that because the biggest waste of money is actually coming from..." On-screen text should condense the spoken hook to 3–5 big words; use staccato short sentences in the hook. — [Kallaway transcript (sozai)](https://sozai.app/transcript/create-irresistible-hooks-blow-content/)
- Hormozi-style hook anatomy (secondary summary, not primary): Call-out (who is this for) + Tension mechanism (curiosity, fear, desire, contrarian, proof) + Payoff promise; types include outcome, time-based, effort-reduction, callout, proof, contrarian, pain, mechanism, transformation. — [hormozi-hooks skill summary](https://skillselion.com/skills/alexsmedile/hormozi-skills/hormozi-hooks) (aggregator; treat as low-confidence)
- Jenny Hoyos: writes ≥10 hooks per idea and has an analyst pick; opens with a question/premise that makes people stay for the answer (more surprising = better); then "constant progression" toward the answer. — [ContentGrip on Hoyos TED talk](https://www.contentgrip.com/how-to-tell-a-great-story-in-60-seconds/); [JXT Group](https://www.jxtgroup.com/?p=3920014)
- Sugarman: "the sole purpose of the first sentence is to get you to read the second sentence" — the "slippery slide". — [The Essence (Substack)](https://theessence.substack.com/p/the-simple-secret-of-really-good)

### Inferences (abstract templates — mechanism from sources above; examples illustrative)
1. **Common belief → "but" → reversal** (Kallaway contrast; Heath "commit to flawed idea"; Kang wrong-guess memory boost). Template: `[What everyone thinks about X]. But [X is actually not the cause / is the least important part].` Ex: "Everyone thinks onions make you cry because of the juice. But the juice is innocent."
2. **Named-mechanism tease** (forward reference + concreteness). `Your [body part/object] does [specific thing] — and it's run by [a mechanism you've never heard named].` Ex: "Your stomach growls even when it's empty — that sound has a name, and it's a cleaning crew."
3. **Specific number anomaly** (numbers ↑CTR; incongruity). `[Ordinary thing] is [surprising number]. That number is the whole story.` Ex: "A cucumber is 96% water. So why doesn't it taste like water?"
4. **Wrong-answer-first ("the answer is not X")** (Muller misconception approach; Kang). `Most people answer [X]. It's not X. It's not Y either.` Ex: "Why do we yawn? Not tiredness. Not oxygen either."
5. **Rank-and-withhold / delayed second reason** (mystery story; open loop). `There are two reasons [X]. The first is obvious. The second is weirder.` Ex: "Cats knock things off tables for two reasons. The second one is about you."
6. **Zoom-in on a micro-moment** (concreteness; social story about an individual). `In the 0.3 seconds after you [everyday act], [specific thing] happens.` Ex: "In the half-second after you stub your toe, your brain is running a race."
7. **Hidden-cost consequence ("you right now")** — use sparingly: Upworthy found reader-directed "you" headlines *backfired* on average, so ground "you" in a concrete action, not flattery. `If you [common habit], [specific consequence] is already happening.` Ex: "If you crack your knuckles, that pop isn't bone — it's a gas bubble collapsing."
8. **Expert-knows-something-you-don't** (Heath: others know). `Dentists notice one thing about [X] that patients never do.`
9. **Prediction request** (Heath: ask for predictions; commitment). `Guess: [quick binary]. Most people get this wrong.` Ex: "Hot water or cold water — which freezes first? Most people pick wrong."
10. **Mystery story / failed theories** (Saturn rings). `For 100 years scientists couldn't explain [X]. Every theory failed — until [clue].`
11. **Contradiction between two true facts** (incongruity). `[Fact A]. And yet [Fact B, which seems to contradict A].` Ex: "Your blood is red. Your veins look blue. Both are true."
12. **Stakes escalation** (Kallaway mortgage; negativity ↑CTR). `[Small everyday thing] — and it's the reason [larger consequence].`
13. **Visual-first proof + verbal gap** (Kallaway alignment; MrBeast "show them the thumbnail promise"). Show the weird thing on screen at 0 s; line says `This shouldn't be possible.` (forward reference "this").

### Gaps
- Primary Hormozi, Dan Koe, Justin Welsh, Lara Acosta hook lists were not retrieved; only an aggregator summary for Hormozi. Their templates are mostly text-post (LinkedIn/X) hooks; treat as unverified for video.
- Ogilvy's headline doctrine, Film Booth, vidIQ, HubSpot, Backlinko lists were not fetched in this pass.
- No study isolates each formula's retention effect in short video; the evidence is headline CTR (first-line choice), not mid-video retention.

## Q2. What does the evidence/consensus say about pairing the hook with an immediate second sentence that *deepens* rather than answers (the "but" sentence)?

### Takeaway
Strong practitioner consensus (Kallaway's lean-in → "but" → snapback; Sugarman's slippery slide; Heath's "open gaps before closing them") is that sentence 2 should *raise* the stakes or contradict sentence 1, not resolve it. The theoretical support is that a reversal converts a vague gap into a specific one and activates the "I guessed wrong" memory/curiosity response.

### Cited Findings
- Kallaway: the interjection line ("but", "however", "yet") "precedes the payoff without carrying the full impact itself"; the snapback then moves opposite to the lean-in. — [Kallaway transcript](https://sozai.app/transcript/create-irresistible-hooks-blow-content/)
- Sugarman: each sentence's job is to get the next one read ("slippery slide"). — [The Essence](https://theessence.substack.com/p/the-simple-secret-of-really-good)
- Heath: highlight what people don't know *then* offer clues; make people commit to a flawed idea before refuting. — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/create-interest)
- Derek Muller's PhD: "clear explanation" physics videos were rated easy and gave confidence but didn't produce learning; videos that voiced the misconception first (actor-student states wrong idea, teacher corrects) roughly doubled post-test scores (to almost 12/26). — [THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx); [Muller, Tackling misconceptions… (Sydney)](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/IISME/article/view/6345)
- Kang 2009: wrong guesses + curiosity → enhanced memory activity for the surprising answer. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/)
- Hoyos "micro-loop": each section ends with a new curiosity gap (e.g., "Before I show you the final result, here's why most people fail at this step…"). — [JXT Group summary](https://www.jxtgroup.com/?p=3920014) (secondary)

### Inferences
- Recommended 2-sentence pattern: S1 = specific, half-known claim (lean-in); S2 = "But/And yet + the obvious explanation is wrong / the visible part is the least interesting part". S2 must not contain the answer; it should narrow the gap ("it's not X") so curiosity rises (Loewenstein: curiosity grows as you get closer).
- A "but" that merely restates S1 is filler; the test is whether S2 *removes* a candidate answer or *adds* a stake.
- Muller's result implies the explainer body should keep voicing a plausible wrong model before each reveal — a natural engine for re-hooks.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment specifically A/B-tests "hook + deepening sentence" vs "hook + answer" in short video; support is theory + practitioner case examples.

## Q3. How should a 60–90 s explainer time its first payoff to avoid "too slow" and "answered too early" drop?

### Takeaway
No rigorous public study gives an optimal timestamp. Converging guidance: win the first 1–3 s (swipe decision), confirm the promise quickly (MrBeast: first stretch must show the thumbnail promise will be met), give a *partial* payoff early, and keep at least one bigger loop open until near the end, re-opening a new gap each time one closes (Hoyos micro-loops; Saturn-ring mystery structure).

### Cited Findings
- MrBeast production guide (leaked 2024, written ~2022): the first minute is where most viewers leave (losing 21M of 60M in minute one counted as "reasonably good"); its job is to show the viewer the title/thumbnail expectation will be met; later segments have distinct jobs (1–3 min "hype to action", 3–6 min most engaging story content). Long-form, but the principle "confirm the promise early, escalate after" transfers. — [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240917-mrbeast-youtube-production); [OMR](https://omr.com/de/daily/mrbeast-doc-pdf-leak); [Creator Handbook](https://www.creatorhandbook.net/leaked-document-allegedly-reveals-mrbeasts-secrets-to-youtube-success-the-key-takeaways/)
- YouTube Shorts "Viewed vs. swiped away" is the first-seconds metric; vendor guidance targets ≥70% viewed and says 30–40%+ instant swipes indicate a failing first 1–3 s; after that, inspect "the first point where the promised payoff is delayed or unclear". — [ContentStudio Shorts analytics](https://contentstudio.io/blog/youtube-shorts-analytics-guide); [Bytecap benchmarks](https://www.bytecap.io/research/youtube-shorts-retention-benchmarks) (vendor benchmarks; methodology not audited)
- Hoyos: "constant progression" toward the answer; micro-loops ending each section with a gap. — [ContentGrip](https://www.contentgrip.com/how-to-tell-a-great-story-in-60-seconds/)
- Information-gap theory: curiosity is strongest at moderate uncertainty — too little or too much information reduces engagement. — [Aubin Le Quéré & Matias 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11704130/)

### Inferences
- Practical schedule for a 60–90 s, ~12–20 sentence explainer: 0–3 s hook (claim/contradiction + visual); 3–8 s "but" deepener; by ~15–20 s a **partial payoff** (name the mechanism or kill the wrong answer) that simultaneously opens the next loop ("but that raises a weirder question"); then a new partial reveal every ~3–4 sentences (~10–15 s); hold the single biggest reveal (or the "you right now" consequence) for the last 15–20 %. The "re-hook every 10–15 s" rule is consistent with this but is practitioner lore, not measured.
- "Answered too early" = the hook question is fully closed with no successor loop; "too slow" = no progress signal (no partial answer, no wrong option eliminated) for >~15 s. Each beat should either close a small gap or eliminate a candidate answer.

### Gaps
- No primary YouTube/TikTok data or peer-reviewed study on optimal payoff timing in shorts was found; "re-hook every 10–15 s" lacks a citable origin.
- Vendor retention benchmarks (70% viewed, etc.) are not independently verified.

## Q4. Is there evidence that a concrete claim or contradiction outperforms a question as a first line? When do (empathy/rhetorical) questions work vs fail?

### Takeaway
Evidence is genuinely mixed. Small early studies found question headlines beat statements on clicks; the largest causal dataset (Upworthy, 22k A/B tests) found interrogative/tutorial cues *reduced* clicks while forward reference, numbers, and concreteness increased them; and a science-news experiment found questions didn't raise clicks but *lowered credibility*. Net: a concrete claim/contradiction is the safer default for an educational channel; questions work only when specific, non-obvious, and self-relevant.

### Cited Findings
- Lai & Farbrot (2013/2014): on a science Twitter feed (6,350 followers), self-referencing question headlines got ~175% more clicks and non-self-referencing questions ~150% more than statements; in a Finn.no marketplace test, 257% / 137% more — but washing-machine questions underperformed, and authors note limited context and that headlines differed beyond question form. — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/are-you-more-likely-click-headlines-are-phrased-question)
- Banerjee & Urminsky (Upworthy, 22,743 RCTs): information-seeking cues (interrogatives, question marks, tutorial words) **decreased** CTR, contrary to prior advice; reader-directed headlines backfired; forward reference, numbers, concreteness increased CTR. — [Working paper](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf)
- KQED science-headline experiment (traditional vs forward-referencing vs question): no significant difference in clicking/engagement intent; question headlines were rated **less credible**, and millennials were more likely to classify them as "fake news" or "satire". — [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/about/13722/experimenting-with-science-news-headline-format-to-maximize-engagement)
- A Radboud thesis on clickbait formulated as statement vs question, with/without exaggeration: headlines without exaggeration were seen as more credible. — [Radboud thesis repository](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/handle/123456789/14307)

### Inferences
- Questions fail when: generic ("Have you ever wondered why…?"), the answer is obvious or guessable, there are no stakes, or the question is a disguised tutorial cue. They work when the question contains a specific incongruity the viewer can't answer ("Why does a cucumber, 96% water, not taste like water?") — i.e., the *question carries a claim*.
- Empathy/공감 questions after a shown scene ("You've done this, right?") are a "lean-in" (Kallaway step 1), not a hook by themselves; they must be followed immediately by the "but" snapback, otherwise they spend the viewer's first seconds on agreement with no gap. Note reader-directed language backfired on average in Upworthy data — keep it tied to a concrete shown action.
- For a knowledge channel where credibility matters, default to a concrete contradiction/claim; reserve questions for prediction-requests (Heath) where the viewer is invited to commit to a wrong answer.

### Gaps
- No short-video (not headline) A/B evidence comparing question vs claim first lines was found. Headline CTR ≠ retention.
- Banerjee & Urminsky numbers quoted are from the SSRN working-paper version; the published 2025 version may differ in detail.

## Q5. What makes a hook feel like clickbait/낚시 and damage trust, and how do top educational creators avoid it? How to turn a mundane observation into a high-stakes, specific hook?

### Takeaway
Clickbait = an open loop the content does not close (a broken promise), vagueness with no informational anchor, exaggeration, and overt manipulation cues. Educational creators (Veritasium) defend "legit bait": enticing but accurate titles that are fully paid off. Specificity (numbers, named mechanism, concrete object/person) both increases clicks up to a point and protects credibility.

### Cited Findings
- Veritasium "Clickbait is Unreasonably Effective" (2021): distinguishes "legit bait" (enticing but accurate) from "click traps" (misleading/low value); argues the former is necessary to reach audiences; the test is whether the promise is kept. — [Briefy summary](https://briefy.ai/summary/v2/curfkxexuv57xsqmhbo9twba/en/clickbait-is-unreasonably-effective); [Boing Boing, "Clickbait isn't the problem: broken promises are"](https://boingboing.net/2025/12/09/clickbait-isnt-the-problem-broken-promises-are.html); [Derek Muller – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Muller)
- Exaggeration lowers perceived credibility of clickbait headlines. — [Radboud thesis](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/handle/123456789/14307)
- Question headlines read as less credible / "fake news" to millennials. — [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/about/13722/experimenting-with-science-news-headline-format-to-maximize-engagement)
- Over-vague headlines ("This Will Change How You Think About…") underperform when competitors are concrete; but over-explicit ones also lose — the sweet spot is moderate information. — [Aubin Le Quéré & Matias 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11704130/)
- Numbers, concreteness, and stories about individuals raise CTR; money mentions and "you"-directed headlines lowered it. — [Banerjee & Urminsky](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf)
- MrBeast guide: the opening must prove the thumbnail/title expectation will be met. — [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240917-mrbeast-youtube-production)
- Negativity raises clicks (+2.3%/negative word) — a lever that, overused, edges into fear-bait; fear specifically *lowered* clicks while sadness raised them. — [PMC10202797](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/)

### Inferences
- Anti-낚시 rules: (a) every loop opened in the first 10 s is explicitly closed on screen before the end; (b) the hook claim must be literally true and the payoff must be *at least as surprising* as promised; (c) prefer an informational anchor in the hook (a number, a named body part/object, a named mechanism) over pronoun-only teasers ("this one thing"); (d) avoid exaggeration words ("insane", "shocking") as substitutes for content; (e) don't use fear for stakes on health topics — use concrete consequence.
- Mundane → high-stakes recipe (synthesis of concreteness + numbers + incongruity + consequence): take the everyday observation ("my fingers wrinkle in the bath") → (1) add a precise number or time ("after about five minutes") → (2) kill the obvious explanation ("it's not water soaking in") → (3) name that a mechanism exists ("it's your nervous system doing it on purpose") → (4) attach a consequence/stakes ("and if it doesn't happen, doctors take note") → (5) keep the "why on purpose" open as the main loop. English example: "Your fingers wrinkle after about five minutes in water. But it's not water soaking in — it's your nerves doing it on purpose."
- "You right now" framing should point at a concrete current sensation or action (blinking, swallowing, posture) rather than generic "you" address, given the Upworthy backfire result.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study on trust damage specifically from short-video hooks (vs headlines) was found; long-term channel-trust effects of clickbait remain undocumented in sources retrieved.
- Primary Veritasium video was not transcribed; claims rely on summaries.
