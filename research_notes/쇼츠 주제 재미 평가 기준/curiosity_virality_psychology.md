# Psychology and Virality Science of Interesting, Curiosity-Inducing, Shareable Short-Video Topics

Scope note: nearly all empirical work below used trivia questions in a lab, text news articles (NYT, Facebook news posts, Upworthy headlines), or ad videos. I found no peer-reviewed study that tests these factors on 60–100 second educational Shorts/Reels/TikTok, and none on a Korean audience. Every application to "100-second Korean body-science Shorts" is an inference.

## 1. Information-gap theory and curiosity vs. prior knowledge (Loewenstein, Kang 2009, Gruber 2014, Metcalfe, Wade & Kidd)

### Takeaway
Curiosity peaks when people feel they *almost* know the answer (moderate confidence, about P≈.5), not when they know nothing or are sure. It is driven by what people *think* they know, and it boosts memory, most of all for answers that overturn a guess. A topic scores best when the average viewer already has a guess or half-knowledge about it and that guess is likely to be partly or entirely wrong.

### Cited Findings
- Kang et al. (2009, *Psychological Science*), Exp. 1: 19 Caltech students in fMRI, 40 trivia questions. Each person guessed silently, then rated curiosity (1–7) and confidence (0–100%). Curiosity followed an inverted U over confidence P and peaked near P=.50. Curiosity correlated with uncertainty P(1−P), r=.44, p<.0005. In three quarters of subjects, peak curiosity fell between P=.45 and .55. — [Kang et al. 2009 PDF (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)
- Kang et al. (2009): curiosity during the question correlated with caudate activity, a region tied to anticipated reward. When a guess was *wrong*, curiosity raised activity in memory regions while the answer was shown. Among items first guessed wrong, recall 1–2 weeks later rose with curiosity: high vs. low curiosity d=1.11, middle vs. low d=0.74, high vs. middle d=0.61. Behavioral follow-ups showed people spent more scarce tokens or waiting time to learn answers they were more curious about. Small samples (19 people in fMRI; about 10 per condition in the token study). — [Kang et al. 2009 PDF (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf); [Berkeley neuroecon summary](https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/papers/papers_files/Kang_PsychSci_2009.html)
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014, *Neuron* 84(2):486–496): memory improved, both immediately and after one day, for trivia answers people were curious about. It also improved for unrelated *incidental* material (faces) shown during high-curiosity states. High curiosity raised activity in the midbrain and nucleus accumbens. Midbrain–hippocampus connectivity predicted individual differences in the incidental-memory benefit. — [PMC4252494](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/); [Cardiff ORCA record](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/96033)
- Metcalfe, Schwartz & Eich (2020), "Epistemic curiosity and the region of proximal learning": curiosity is lowest when people are sure they know and highest when they believe they "almost know." When no feedback was given, people were *more curious about high-confidence errors* than about equally confident correct answers. — [FIU Discovery record](https://discovery.fiu.edu/display/pub101464); [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10486440)
- Wade & Kidd (2019): 114 participants, 100 trivia questions. Curiosity was predicted by people's *perceived* closeness to the answer, not by their objective prior knowledge; it peaked when they felt almost but not completely sure. Learning was predicted by both curiosity and objective prior knowledge. — [Berkeley News 2019](https://news.berkeley.edu/2019/05/23/curiositystudy)
- Brod, Hasselhorn & Bunge (2018, *Learning and Instruction* 55:22–31): making a prediction (compared with judging the outcome after the fact) improved learning of geography facts. Pupil-measured surprise at expectation-violating outcomes appeared only when a prediction had been made, and stronger surprise went with more learning. A "Lighting the wick" follow-up found that generating a prediction itself increases curiosity. — [Fachportal record](https://www.fachportal-paedagogik.de/literatur/vollanzeige.html?FId=A34255); [PMC6803639](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6803639/)
- Dubey, Griffiths & Lombrozo (2022, *Cognition* 226:105193): telling people that knowledge about a scientific topic is useful, personally or to society, increased curiosity and later information search. "Simply presenting interesting facts is not enough to influence curiosity." — [Princeton PDF](https://cognition.princeton.edu/sites/default/files/dubey_-_if_its_important_then_im_curious_increasing_perceived_usefulness_stimulates_curiosity_0.pdf)

### Inferences
- Topic-level translation: the viewer should be able to form a guess at once ("I think I know why my stomach growls…"). Topics so obscure that no guess is possible (an unfamiliar enzyme name) sit at the low end of the inverted U. Topics everyone already knows ("water is good for you") sit at the satiated end.
- What counts is the viewer's *perceived* closeness (Wade & Kidd), so a topic framed around a familiar phenomenon with a hidden "why" beats one that needs prior jargon.
- Usefulness raises curiosity on its own (Dubey et al.). For science content, curiosity and practical value are not independent axes; they add to each other.
- A Short that opens with an implicit "guess what…" makes viewers predict, which raises surprise and retention (Brod et al.). That is a script tactic, but it means topics that *allow* a prediction are better raw material.

### Gaps
- All of these are lab trivia paradigms with small samples (Kang n=19 in fMRI). I found no test of the inverted U on video watch-time or retention curves.
- No cross-cultural (Korean) replication found.

## 2. Surprise, expectation violation and misconception correction (hypercorrection)

### Takeaway
Errors held with high confidence are corrected *more* easily than low-confidence errors, because the clash between confidence and the true answer draws attention. People are also more curious about such errors. Popular misconceptions ("we only use 10% of our brain") make strong topics, provided the correction is solid and feels sound.

### Cited Findings
- Hypercorrection effect: high-confidence errors on general-knowledge questions are more likely to be corrected after feedback than low-confidence errors (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001). The finding has been replicated many times. — [Duke: Understanding the Hypercorrection Effect](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/d6ab09ca-f55c-4efb-9d32-2788bc5838b2/full); [Columbia Academic Commons](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/j6d6-cq62)
- Mechanism: a metacognitive mismatch (surprise) between confidence and the true answer increases attention to the correction. Butterfield & Metcalfe (2006) used a concurrent tone-detection task and found attention selectively captured during feedback on high-confidence errors. — [Duke dissertation](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/d6ab09ca-f55c-4efb-9d32-2788bc5838b2); [PMC4036076](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4036076)
- Curiosity is higher for high-confidence errors than for equally confident correct answers (Metcalfe et al. 2020, see §1). — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10486440)
- In Kang et al. (2009), answers that followed *incorrect* guesses produced extra memory-region activity, and curiosity predicted their later recall. The authors read this as curiosity enhancing memory for surprising new information. — [Kang et al. 2009 PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)
- Surprise as a sharing driver: a one-SD rise in coded surprise raised the probability of making the NYT most-emailed list by about 14% (Berger & Milkman 2012, see §3). A small analysis of 9 viral marketing campaigns (Dobele et al. 2007) found surprise was the only emotion present in all nine. — [Berger & Milkman 2012 PDF](https://qubeshub.org/app/site/collections/4299/Berger__Milkman.pdf); [New Republic summary of Dobele 2007](https://newrepublic.com/article/120331/what-makes-something-go-viral)
- Children's learning from being proven wrong depended on executive-function skills. The prediction-error benefit is not universal. — [Frankfurt publication record](https://publikationen.ub.uni-frankfurt.de/frontdoor/index/index/year/2020/docId/55895)

### Inferences
- A topic built on a *widely and confidently held* belief that turns out wrong or half-wrong is the highest-yield form. Examples: "cracking knuckles causes arthritis," "you lose most heat through your head," "yawning is contagious because of oxygen." It hits curiosity (high-confidence error), attention (mismatch) and memory (hypercorrection) at once.
- Correction topics need a clear, verifiable correct answer. Hypercorrection works on feedback, so a vague "it's complicated" ending loses the effect.
- Surprise has a smaller *sharing* effect (+14%) than awe or practical value (+30%), but a large *attention and memory* effect. For Shorts, where retention drives distribution, surprise may matter more than the NYT email data suggest (inference, untested).

### Gaps
- Hypercorrection studies use brief textual feedback on lab trivia. I found no study of misconception-correction *videos* and sharing.
- Risk of the backfire or familiarity effect (repeating a myth strengthens it) was not researched here. The report writer should flag it as a script-level concern.

## 3. Berger & Milkman (2012) and STEPPS: emotion, practical value, social currency

### Takeaway
In about 7,000 NYT articles, high-arousal emotion (awe, anger, anxiety) and practical utility each raised the odds of being widely emailed by about 20–34% per SD; interest raised them about 25% and surprise about 14%. Sadness *lowered* them. For body-science content, awe ("your body does this incredible thing") and practical utility are the most usable levers. Anger fits poorly, and anxiety is usable only in small doses.

### Cited Findings
- Data: 6,956 NYT articles published Aug 30–Nov 30, 2008. Outcome: whether an article made the "most e-mailed" list. Model: logistic regression with home-page placement, timing, word count, author fame and gender, and day fixed effects as controls. Emotions were hand-coded on a subset (n=2,566). — [Berger & Milkman 2012, *JMR* PDF](https://qubeshub.org/app/site/collections/4299/Berger__Milkman.pdf)
- Percentage change in the fitted probability of making the list for +1 SD (Figure 2): anger +34%, awe +30%, practical value +30%, interest +25%, anxiety +21%, emotionality +18%, surprise +14%, positivity +13%, sadness −16%. For comparison, +1 SD more time at the top of the home page gave +20%. — [Berger & Milkman 2012 PDF](https://qubeshub.org/app/site/collections/4299/Berger__Milkman.pdf)
- Coefficients (Table 5, Model 4 with controls): awe .34, anger .38, anxiety .24, sadness −.17, practical utility .34, interest .29, surprise .16 (all p<.05 or better). McFadden R² went from .07 (emotions only) to .28 with controls. The results held within topic sections; the authors note that even among opinion or *health* articles, awe-inspiring ones were more viral. — [Berger & Milkman 2012 PDF](https://qubeshub.org/app/site/collections/4299/Berger__Milkman.pdf)
- Experiments (Studies 2a/2b): manipulating amusement or anger raised sharing intentions, and self-reported arousal mediated the anger effect. — [Berger & Milkman 2012 PDF](https://qubeshub.org/app/site/collections/4299/Berger__Milkman.pdf)
- Berger (2011, *Psychological Science*): physiological arousal from any source raised sharing. 75% of participants who had jogged in place forwarded an article, against 33% of those who sat still. — [New Republic summary](https://newrepublic.com/article/120331/what-makes-something-go-viral)
- Video evidence: Nelson-Field, Riebe & Newstead (2013, *Australasian Marketing Journal*) took 800 videos with 3 to 109,000 daily Facebook shares. 28 participants watched 50 videos each and named their emotion. High-arousal videos were shared about twice as often, and positive videos got about 30% more shares than negative ones. — [New Republic summary](https://newrepublic.com/article/120331/what-makes-something-go-viral)
- Health news specifically: Hyun Suk Kim's analysis of NYT health articles found that high informational utility and positive sentiment predicted both selection (clicks) and retransmission. Controversial, emotionally evocative and *familiar* content was more "attractable" (clicked). Utility and novelty mattered more for email sharing; emotion, familiarity and exemplification (concrete cases) mattered more for social-media sharing. — [Kim dissertation record (UPenn)](https://upenn-test.atmire.com/entities/person/66a46e5c-a2b8-49aa-836a-48f4d6879067)
- Scholz et al. (2017, *PNAS*): expected *self-related* outcomes (for example, looking knowledgeable) and *social* outcomes of sharing feed a common neural value signal (vmPFC), which predicted population-level virality of NYT health articles. — [PMC5358393](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5358393); [Stanford PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/bad/scholz17.pdf)
- Berger & Milkman's own theory note: people share interesting or surprising content partly because it "reflects positively on them" (signals that they know interesting things). This is the social-currency mechanism. — [Berger & Milkman 2012 PDF](https://qubeshub.org/app/site/collections/4299/Berger__Milkman.pdf)
- Conflicting secondary report: one search summary claimed awe raises virality by "34 percent." The primary paper says anger +34% and awe +30%. Use the primary figures. — [Berger & Milkman 2012 PDF](https://qubeshub.org/app/site/collections/4299/Berger__Milkman.pdf)

### Inferences (STEPPS applied to body science)
- **Social currency**: strong fit. "Did you know…" body facts make the sharer look knowledgeable (Scholz self-related value; Berger & Milkman interest +25%).
- **Triggers**: strong fit (see §4). Daily bodily experiences act as built-in cues.
- **Emotion**: awe (scale and precision of the body) and mild anxiety (a common habit is quietly harming you) fit. Anger rarely fits body facts. Sadness (disease tragedy) should be avoided, since it lowers sharing.
- **Public**: low relevance at the topic level.
- **Practical value**: strong fit, with an effect size equal to awe (+30%) and reinforced by Kim's health-news findings.
- **Stories**: Kim's "exemplification" (concrete cases) helped social sharing. Topics with a concrete case or experiment beat abstract mechanisms.
- STEPPS itself is a framework from Berger's book *Contagious* (2013), synthesized from his studies. It is not a single validated scale, so its six parts should not be read as having equal or measured weights.

### Gaps
- The effect sizes come from text articles shared by email in 2008. Shorts are distributed by the recommendation algorithm (watch time, swipe-away rate), not by user forwarding. I found no study that maps these features onto YouTube Shorts metrics.
- I did not fetch the Nelson-Field 2013 paper directly; its numbers come from a magazine summary.
- I found no effect sizes for "Triggers" or "Stories" on article or video sharing.

## 4. Self-relevance and triggers

### Takeaway
Content tied to things people meet often (daily bodily sensations, seasonal events) stays mentally accessible, and that accessibility drives *ongoing* word of mouth. Self-relevance also directly raises the neural value of sharing. Body topics have a structural edge: the trigger is the viewer's own body.

### Cited Findings
- Berger & Schwartz (2011, *JMR*), "What Drives Immediate and Ongoing Word of Mouth?": accessibility (how often cues in the environment bring a product to mind) drives ongoing word of mouth, while interestingness drives mainly immediate word of mouth. The BzzAgent field data are in the working paper. — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/10/BzzAgent.pdf); [secondary summary](https://adigaskell.org/?p=694)
- Scholz et al. (2017): activity in self-related brain regions (mPFC, PCC) during reading predicted sharing and population virality. Self-relevance is one of two main inputs to the value of sharing. — [PMC5358393](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5358393)
- Familiar content was more clickable in NYT health news (Kim). — [Kim dissertation record](https://upenn-test.atmire.com/entities/person/66a46e5c-a2b8-49aa-836a-48f4d6879067)
- Dubey et al. (2022): perceived personal and social usefulness of a science topic raised curiosity. — [Princeton PDF](https://cognition.princeton.edu/sites/default/files/dubey_-_if_its_important_then_im_curious_increasing_perceived_usefulness_stimulates_curiosity_0.pdf)

### Inferences
- High-trigger body topics: things the viewer will feel again within days. Examples: hiccups, yawning, goosebumps, pins and needles, stomach growling, eye floaters, "brain freeze," morning stiffness, waking just before the alarm. Each recurrence reminds viewers of the video and is a moment when they may talk about it.
- Seasonal triggers matter for a Korean audience (inference): 환절기 colds, 장마 joint pain, summer heat and sweating, winter static electricity and dry skin, 김장 season, 수능 stress, 명절 overeating. These raise accessibility for a time window.
- Self-relevance at the topic level: "your body, right now" beats "rare disease X" or "astronaut bodies," unless the latter carry awe.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the full Berger & Schwartz paper to get its effect sizes for accessibility vs. interest. The 15% figure in a secondary source refers to branded freebies and is not clearly a trigger effect.
- I found no research on seasonal triggers for health or science video content.

## 5. Clickbait and curiosity gaps: when they backfire

### Takeaway
Curiosity gaps work only in moderation, and only when the payoff is delivered. The large Upworthy A/B data show a *curvilinear* relation: too little or too much information in a headline both hurt. Stock clickbait phrases *reduced* engagement on Facebook. Clickbait tends to lower perceived credibility, though the measured effects are small and mixed.

### Cited Findings
- Aubin Le Quéré (Cornell), "When Curiosity Gaps Backfire": in the Upworthy Research Archive of A/B-tested headlines, headline concreteness had a curvilinear relation with engagement; "there can be too much, or too little, information conveyed in a headline." — [Cambridge talk abstract](https://talks.cam.ac.uk/talk/index/210730/)
- Jung, Stieglitz, Kissmer, Mirbabaie & Kroll (2022, *PLoS One*): 4,410 Facebook posts from 10 news outlets (5 reputable, 5 tabloid). Headlines with common clickbait phrases ("will blow your mind") got about 25–30% *fewer* reactions, shares and comments, contrary to the authors' hypothesis. Unusual punctuation in headlines raised engagement (up to +88.9% shares) but lowered it in post text (−44% shares). Shorter headline words raised engagement. The authors describe the literature as showing a "broadly negative impact on audience perceptions, though the effects are small." — [PMC9242456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9242456/)
- Molyneux & Coddington (2020, *Journalism Practice*): two online experiments found clickbait headlines may lower perceived credibility and quality. — [Temple ScholarShare](https://scholarshare.temple.edu/entities/publication/0f000984-ccd7-461a-881c-6b8df9539452)
- Munger (clickbait experiments): no evidence that reading clickbait headlines changed affective polarization, information retention or trust in media. Taken with the studies above, this suggests the effects of clickbait are context-dependent and often null. — [CITP talk summary](https://citp.princeton.edu/events/2018/citp-luncheon-speaker-series-kevin-munger-%E2%80%93-clickbait)
- Question-based headlines drew more negative reader reactions and expectations than traditional headlines. Forward-reference headlines with a curiosity gap did *not* differ from traditional ones. — [journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/readers-perceive-question-based-headlines-more-negatively-study-shows/)
- Theory link: Silvia's appraisal theory (§6) predicts that novelty without comprehensibility yields confusion rather than interest. An over-promised gap that the content cannot close is the same failure at the level of expectations. — [Silvia & Kashdan 2009](https://mason.gmu.edu/~tkashdan/publications/silvia%20kashdan%20compass%202009.pdf)

### Inferences
- Topic scoring should penalize topics whose "hook" over-promises what 100 seconds and the evidence can support. Examples: "this one food cures X," a "secret" mechanism science hasn't settled, or a sensational claim that needs heavy hedging.
- Topics where the answer is *surprising and true and clearly explainable* let the hook be bold without being deceptive. That is the ideal intersection.
- For a channel (repeat viewers, subscriptions), the long-run trust cost of over-promising matters more than for one-off news clicks (inference).

### Gaps
- All of these studies are on headlines or text news. I found no study of curiosity-gap *video hooks* or thumbnails and retention or dislikes.
- The Upworthy curvilinear study's effect sizes were not in the abstract.

## 6. Interestingness of facts: Silvia's appraisal theory (novelty-complexity + coping potential)

### Takeaway
Interest arises when something seems new, complex or unexpected *and* the person feels able to understand it. Either alone fails: novelty without comprehensibility produces confusion, and comprehensibility without novelty produces boredom. For a 100-second Short, a topic must be explainable to a lay viewer within that time.

### Cited Findings
- Silvia's appraisal model: interest comes from two appraisals, novelty-complexity (new, unexpected, complex, unfamiliar) and coping potential (feeling able to understand it). Individual differences in interest reflect differences in these appraisals. — [Silvia & Kashdan 2009, *Social and Personality Psychology Compass*](https://mason.gmu.edu/~tkashdan/publications/silvia%20kashdan%20compass%202009.pdf); [Knowledge Emotions chapter](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/testbookje/?p=456)
- Interest and confusion are distinct knowledge emotions. Confusion arises when novelty is appraised as *not* comprehensible. — [Interest and Confusion: Contrasting Theoretical Approaches](https://sonar.unidistance.ch/documents/336002/files/Interest-and-Confusion-Contrasting-Theoretical-Approaches.pdf)
- Interest was a separate positive predictor of virality in the NYT data (+25% per SD), alongside emotion. Coded text "complexity" was not significant (coef .05). — [Berger & Milkman 2012 PDF](https://qubeshub.org/app/site/collections/4299/Berger__Milkman.pdf)
- The information-gap inverted U (§1) agrees with Silvia: too little prior knowledge (no coping potential) and too much (no novelty) both reduce curiosity. — [Kang et al. 2009 PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)

### Inferences
- Topics need a mechanism that can be shown with one or two visual analogies in about 100 seconds. "Why do fingers wrinkle in water" (one mechanism, testable at home) beats "how the immune system's complement cascade works."
- Coping potential depends on the audience. For a general Korean audience, topics that need Latin or hanja medical terms to state should be penalized unless the term itself is the hook.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative work rating the "interestingness" of individual science facts across large item sets with predictive validity for video performance.

## Proposed scoring dimensions (1/3/5 anchors) for an LLM to rate a candidate topic

Each dimension combines factors that evidence shows act together. An equal-weight sum is a reasonable default; the evidence does not support precise weights. Berger & Milkman's per-SD effects are similar in size (about +14% to +34%), and they come from text and email sharing, not Shorts.

### A. Knowledge gap / misconception tension (지식 간극·오개념)
Evidence: inverted U of curiosity vs. confidence (Kang 2009; Wade & Kidd 2019); "almost know" (Metcalfe 2020); hypercorrection and high-confidence-error curiosity (Butterfield & Metcalfe; Metcalfe 2020); prediction and surprise boost learning (Brod 2018).
- **1**: The viewer has no basis for a guess (obscure jargon or a phenomenon they have never noticed), *or* the answer is already common knowledge ("smoking is bad for the lungs").
- **3**: The viewer has noticed the phenomenon and has a vague guess, but there is no clear wrong belief to overturn ("why do we have eyebrows?").
- **5**: Most viewers hold a *confident, specific* belief that is wrong or importantly incomplete, and the topic can state the correct answer clearly ("cracking knuckles causes arthritis → it doesn't"; "you lose most body heat through your head → no").

### B. Self-relevance and practical value (나와의 관련성·실용성·트리거)
Evidence: practical utility +30% (Berger & Milkman 2012); utility predicted selection and sharing of health news (Kim); self-related value predicts virality (Scholz 2017); perceived usefulness raises curiosity (Dubey 2022); accessibility and triggers drive ongoing word of mouth (Berger & Schwartz 2011).
- **1**: About bodies or conditions the viewer will rarely if ever experience, with nothing to apply ("bodily changes in astronauts after a year in orbit," framed with no link to the viewer).
- **3**: A phenomenon the viewer experiences sometimes, *or* a useful takeaway that is generic ("drink enough water").
- **5**: A sensation or habit the viewer meets daily or seasonally (a built-in trigger), plus a concrete takeaway they can try or tell someone today ("why you get hiccups, and what actually stops them"; 환절기, 장마, or 명절 timing).

### C. Emotional arousal: awe or surprise, not sadness (정서적 각성: 경이·놀라움)
Evidence: awe +30%, anxiety +21%, surprise +14%, sadness −16% (Berger & Milkman 2012); high-arousal videos shared about 2× more (Nelson-Field 2013); arousal itself raises sharing (Berger 2011); social currency (Scholz 2017; Berger & Milkman).
- **1**: Emotionally flat or low-arousal ("the liver has many functions"), or mainly sad or tragic (disease suffering), which lowers sharing.
- **3**: Mildly amusing or surprising; viewers would say "huh, neat" but would not rush to tell anyone.
- **5**: Strong awe at scale, speed or precision ("your stomach makes a new lining every few days so it doesn't digest itself"), or a jolt of surprise or mild, actionable concern. Viewers would want to say "did you know…" to someone. Avoid fear without a resolution.

### D. Payoff deliverability and honesty (설명 가능성·약속 이행)
Evidence: interest needs novelty *and* coping potential (Silvia); curvilinear curiosity-gap effect (Upworthy, Aubin Le Quéré); stock clickbait lowered engagement (Jung et al. 2022); clickbait lowers credibility (Molyneux & Coddington 2020); hypercorrection needs clear corrective feedback.
- **1**: The hook promises more than the evidence or 100 seconds can deliver. The answer is unsettled, needs heavy hedging, rests on weak or single studies, or needs extensive jargon. Any honest title would be clickbait.
- **3**: The answer is well established but takes several mechanisms or terms to explain. It fits in 100 seconds only with simplification, and the ending is a little anticlimactic.
- **5**: One clear, well-supported mechanism that can be shown with one or two visuals or analogies. It can be fully resolved in 100 seconds, and the true answer is itself the surprising payoff, so a bold hook stays honest.

Usage note (inference): A and C mostly predict whether the hook stops the scroll and whether people share; B predicts ongoing recall and sharing (triggers); D works as a gate on long-run trust and watch-through. One option is to treat D≤2 as a veto rather than averaging it in, since over-promising undercuts the other three dimensions (Silvia's "both appraisals required" logic).
